Why the scheduler rewrite paid off
A field report from twelve production clusters, and what it cost us to find out.
The scheduler rewrite started as a boring question: how much of our p99 tail came from the per-request lock rather than from the storage layer behind it? We instrumented 12 production clusters for six weeks before changing a single line of code, and the answer was uncomfortable.
Throughput rose 41% once the lock became a sharded queue, but only after we stopped letting the queue grow without a bound. Keeping the queue shallow and shedding load into a retry budget turned out to matter more than the sharding itself. The full write-up lives at example.com/systems-report.
What broke on the way
Two regressions followed the rollout and both were our own fault. A stale shard counter made the autoscaler over-provision for 40 minutes, and a missing deadline on the drain path turned a routine deploy into a 12 minute p99 spike that nobody paged on, because the alert still watched the old metric.
The honest summary is that the queue was never the bottleneck: it was the only place we looked. A latency budget per request path, written down and enforced in review, did more for the tail than any code we shipped in this rewrite.
